The Best Yarn for Baby Blankets: Soft, Washable and Safe

For a baby blanket that will actually get used, choose a machine-washable, tumble-dryable, weight-4 yarn in acrylic, a cotton-acrylic blend, or superwash merino. That is the whole answer. Everything below is about why, and about the trade-off you are making when you pick something else.

The four properties that matter, in order

Softness is fourth on this list, which surprises people. Here is the order that survives contact with a real household.

1. It must survive a washing machine

A baby blanket is going to be washed constantly, at short notice, by an exhausted person who is not going to read a care label at 3am. A yarn that needs hand-washing and flat-drying is a yarn that will be ruined, and then quietly retired to a cupboard.

This single property eliminates most untreated wool, most alpaca, most silk and most novelty fibres. It is not a small filter. It is the filter.

2. It must not shrink or felt

Untreated animal fibres felt when agitated in warm water — the scales on the fibre lock together and the fabric becomes smaller, denser and permanently stiff. A felted blanket cannot be recovered.

“Superwash” wool has been treated to prevent this and is machine washable. Ordinary merino is not, however soft it feels in the shop.

3. It must not shed or pill badly

Anything that comes off the blanket ends up near a baby’s face. Very fuzzy chenille and eyelash-style yarns shed noticeably, and they pill into hard little balls after a few washes. They photograph beautifully when new. They are the yarns people most regret.

4. Then, softness

Once the first three are satisfied, pick the softest option you can afford. Not before.

The case for acrylic, which people are snobbish about

Acrylic is the most common choice for baby blankets and it deserves its place. It survives a hot wash and a tumble dryer, it costs a fraction of wool, it comes in every colour, it does not felt, and — the underrated part — it can be replaced. If the blanket is loved to destruction, another skein of the same yarn will still be on the shelf in two years. That is not true of a hand-dyed limited run.

Modern baby-specific acrylics are also genuinely soft. The 1980s scratchy acrylic that gave the fibre its reputation is not what you will find in a baby yarn today.

The case against chenille and velvet yarns

They are the most-photographed baby blanket yarns on the internet and the most complained-about in use. They shed fibres, they “worm” (individual stitches twist out of the fabric surface), and the shedding is the reason to think hard before using them for something that sits near a baby’s face.

If you want the look, use a velvet yarn for a toy or a cushion, not a blanket.

What about wool sensitivity?

True wool allergy is rare. What is common is irritation from coarse fibre diameter — the prickle you feel from a scratchy jumper is mechanical, not allergic, and fine merino usually does not cause it where coarse wool does.

But “usually” is doing work in that sentence, and you will not be there when the blanket is used. For a gift, when you do not know the family, choose acrylic or a cotton blend. For your own child, whose skin you know, wool is a reasonable choice.

The one thing to check on the label

The care symbols, before you buy — not the fibre name. Two yarns of the same fibre from different brands can have entirely different care instructions depending on the treatment.

What you want to see: machine wash, and ideally tumble dry low. What you do not want: a hand-wash symbol, or “dry flat” without a machine-wash symbol next to it.

Then buy all the yarn at once, in the same dye lot. Dye lots shift, and a shift halfway across a blanket shows.

What most yarn guides get wrong

They rank by softness first. Softness in the shop is the least reliable predictor of whether the blanket is still in use in a year.

They treat “natural” as a synonym for “better for babies”. Untreated wool is natural and it felts in a hot wash. Alpaca is natural and it is too warm for an infant. Bamboo viscose is heavily processed and is one of the nicest baby fibres available. The natural/synthetic axis is not the useful one.

They rarely mention replaceability, which for a blanket that gets destroyed and mourned is a real feature.

Frequently asked questions

What is the softest yarn for a baby blanket? Superwash merino and bamboo-blend yarns feel softest. Baby-specific acrylics are close behind at a fraction of the price and are more practical.

Is cotton good for a baby blanket? Yes, particularly for warm climates and summer. It is cooler, very durable and washes well. It is heavier than acrylic and has less stretch, so it drapes rather than snuggles.

How much does yarn for a baby blanket cost? For a 30 × 35 inch blanket you need roughly 1,000–1,150 yards. In mid-range acrylic that is around three skeins. In hand-dyed superwash merino the same blanket can cost ten times as much.

Can I use two different yarns in one blanket? Only if they have the same care requirements and similar weight. A blanket that is half superwash and half untreated wool will distort in its first wash.

What yarn weight should I use? Weight 4, medium/worsted, for most baby blankets. See the yarn weight chart.
