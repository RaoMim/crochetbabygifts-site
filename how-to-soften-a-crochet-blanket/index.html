How to Soften a Stiff Crochet Blanket

Wash it first. A blanket that has never been washed is carrying spinning oils and mill finish, and a single gentle wash and low tumble dry softens most of them more than any treatment you can buy. Do that before spending money or taking a risk.

If it is still stiff after a wash, the cause is one of three things, and they need completely different responses — including one that no amount of washing will touch.

Work out which problem you have

Cause How to tell What fixes it Reversible?
Never washed or blocked It is new, or straight off the hook A wash, then low tumble dry or wet blocking Yes
Stiff fibre Cheap acrylic, 100% cotton, or linen; the yarn was stiff on the ball too Conditioner soak, softener, repeated washing Yes
Tension too tight The fabric is dense and barely bends; you can hardly see through it against a light Nothing. It is structural No — it must be remade
Acrylic with no drape Soft to touch but the whole blanket holds its shape like card Steaming, which permanently relaxes the fibre No — permanent

The tight-tension case is the one worth identifying early, because every softening method on the internet will be tried on it and none of them will work. Stiffness from tension is not on the surface of the fabric; it is the fabric. Hold it up to a window: a blanket with healthy drape lets light through the stitches and folds under its own weight. One made at too tight a gauge does neither, and the only real fix is a bigger hook and a fresh start.

The wash, done properly

The default fix, and it is free.

Wash on a gentle cycle in cool to warm water — up to about 30°C / 86°F — with a small amount of mild detergent. Less detergent, not more. Residue is stiffening in its own right, and a heavily soaped wash on a thick blanket does not rinse out fully.

Then run an extra rinse. On a dense crocheted fabric this does more than the wash itself.

Dry on low heat with a couple of wool dryer balls if the fibre permits a dryer at all. The mechanical knocking against the balls is doing real work here, breaking the fabric’s set. Check the care symbols first — see machine-washable yarn for which fibres tolerate this.

The vinegar rinse

About half a cup of plain white vinegar in the rinse cycle, in place of any softener.

The mechanism is straightforward: detergent is alkaline, and alkaline residue left in fibres is one of the reasons a washed item still feels harsh. An acid rinse neutralises it and helps it wash clear. It is not a softener in the fabric-softener sense; it is a better rinse.

The smell goes as it dries. Do not use it on wool blends without checking the label, and never mix it with bleach.

The conditioner soak

The technique that works best on acrylic, and the one most people have not heard of.

Fill a basin with cool water, dissolve a couple of tablespoons of ordinary hair conditioner, submerge the blanket, and leave it 20 to 30 minutes. Squeeze — do not wring — rinse briefly, roll in a towel to remove water, and dry flat or on low heat.

It works because hair conditioner and fabric softener are the same class of chemistry: positively-charged surfactants that deposit a thin lubricating film on the fibre. On acrylic, which holds that film well, the difference is obvious after one soak. The advantage over commercial softener is that you control the dose and can pick something unscented.

The trade-off, stated plainly: you are coating the fibres. That reduces absorbency, and on anything meant to soak up liquid — a washcloth, a bib, a burp cloth — this is the wrong treatment. On a blanket, absorbency does not matter and drape does.

Blocking

Wet blocking relaxes and evens the fabric and improves drape, and it is the standard finishing step that home-made items most often skip.

Soak the blanket, squeeze out the water without wringing, lay it flat on towels, pat it to the dimensions you want, and leave it to dry completely.

It works well on wool and cotton and poorly on acrylic, which has no memory to set — an acrylic blanket springs back to its previous state as it dries. That difference is exactly why the next section exists.

Steaming acrylic — permanent, so read this twice

Acrylic is a thermoplastic. Heat relaxes it, and when it cools it holds the new state permanently. Crocheters call this “killing” the acrylic, which is an accurate name: the fabric goes from stiff and springy to soft and fluid, and it never goes back.

Never let the iron touch the fabric. Acrylic melts. Hold a steam iron one to two inches above the surface, move it steadily, and let the steam do it. Work in sections and let each cool before handling.

Test on a corner or a leftover swatch first, and do not do this at all to anything that needs to hold its structure — a hat, a basket, a bootie, anything with shaping. Killed acrylic drapes beautifully and loses its stitch definition and its bounce along with the stiffness. For a flat blanket that is a good trade. For most other things it is not.

What most guides get wrong

They give one fix for four different problems. “Wash it with fabric softener” is offered as the answer regardless of whether the blanket is unwashed, made of stiff cotton, worked at too tight a gauge, or simply acrylic. It only actually solves one of those.

They do not tell you that tension cannot be softened. This is the single most useful thing to know before starting, because it saves an afternoon of treatments that were never going to work.

They recommend fabric softener on baby items without the caveat. Softener leaves a deliberate residue and usually a fragrance, on an item that will be against a newborn’s face. A vinegar rinse and a thorough second rinse gets much of the benefit with nothing left behind. If you do use softener, use half what the bottle says and rinse again.

They describe steaming acrylic as a softening tip rather than as an irreversible alteration. It is closer to dyeing than to washing. It is a good technique, correctly applied, and it deserves the warning.

Frequently asked questions

Will a stiff blanket soften on its own? Gradually, over many washes and a lot of use. If it is a gift and you want it soft now, use the wash-plus-conditioner route rather than waiting.

Can I soften cotton? Yes, and cotton improves more with washing than any other fibre here — the first three or four washes make a real difference. Skip the conditioner soak if the item is meant to absorb water.

Does fabric softener damage yarn? Not structurally, but it builds up over time, dulls colour slightly, and reduces absorbency. On acrylic it is essentially permanent build-up. Use it sparingly.

Why did my blanket get stiffer after washing? Almost always detergent residue, or drying flat in a heap so fibres set hard. Rewash with no detergent, rinse twice, and dry with movement rather than in a lump.

Is a softened blanket still safe for a baby? The softening does not change the safe-sleep position — no loose blanket in the sleep space under twelve months, regardless of how soft it is. See are crochet blankets safe for babies.
