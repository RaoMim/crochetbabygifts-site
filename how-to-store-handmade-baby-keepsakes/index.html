How to Store Handmade Baby Keepsakes So They Last

Wash it, dry it completely, fold it with acid-free tissue, put it in a cotton bag or a cardboard archive box, and store it somewhere inside the house at a stable temperature. Not the loft. Not the garage. Not a sealed plastic tub.

That last part is where most keepsakes are lost, and it is the opposite of what feels safest.

What actually destroys textiles in storage

Four things, in order of how much damage they do:

1. Body oils and invisible residue. Milk, sweat and skin oils that look like nothing on a clean-looking blanket oxidise over years into brown stains that cannot be removed, and they attract insects. This is the number one killer of stored baby textiles, and it is why washing first is not optional.

2. Trapped moisture. A sealed plastic container holds whatever humidity was in the room when you closed it. Textiles need to breathe; sealed plastic gives mildew a stable home.

3. Temperature cycling. A loft swings from freezing to 50°C across a year. Every cycle expands and contracts the fibres. Lofts and garages are the two worst places in a house for textiles and they are where almost everyone puts them.

4. Light and acid. Sunlight fades and weakens fibres. Ordinary cardboard and tissue are acidic and will yellow whatever touches them over a decade.

Insects are a real fifth risk, but almost entirely for wool — moths and carpet beetles eat protein fibres. They have little interest in acrylic or cotton except as somewhere to nest.

The method

1. Wash it, even if it looks clean. Gentle cycle, mild fragrance-free detergent, no fabric softener. See how to wash it for the fibre-by-fibre settings.

2. Dry it completely. Longer than you think — a thick blanket can feel dry and still hold moisture at the centre. Give it an extra day flat. Any residual damp becomes mildew.

3. Do not starch it and do not use fabric softener. Both leave a residue that attracts insects and discolours over time.

4. Fold with acid-free tissue between the layers, and pad the folds so the creases are soft rather than sharp. A sharp fold held for twenty years becomes a permanent weak line where the fibres break.

Rolling around a cardboard tube covered in acid-free tissue is better still, because there are no folds at all. It takes more space.

5. Put it in something breathable. In order of preference: an acid-free archive box, a cotton pillowcase or cotton storage bag, or a plain cardboard box lined with acid-free tissue. Not a vacuum bag — the compression permanently crushes crochet texture.

6. Store it inside the house. A wardrobe shelf, under a bed, a spare-room cupboard. Anywhere that stays roughly at living temperature and out of direct sun.

7. Label it. Baby’s name, date, who made it, what fibre it is. In twenty years the fibre will matter for washing it and nobody will remember.

8. Refold it every couple of years, along different lines. This moves the stress points and takes two minutes.

Cost, honestly

Acid-free tissue and a proper archive box come to roughly $15–25 and will outlast the blanket. A cotton pillowcase you already own and a cardboard box come to nothing and get you most of the way — the wash, the dryness, and being indoors are worth far more than the container.

If you do only one thing: wash it and keep it inside the house. That is most of the benefit.

Cedar and mothballs

Cedar is mildly repellent to moths when fresh and loses effect within a year or two unless sanded. It does not kill anything. Harmless, and not a plan on its own.

Mothballs work and smell permanently, and the smell transfers to the textile. For wool being stored for decades in a moth-prone house they are a real option. For an acrylic or cotton baby blanket they are unnecessary — moths are not interested.

Lavender sachets are pleasant and do essentially nothing against insects. Use them because you like them.

If it is already stained or yellowed

Old oxidised stains are often permanent, but they are worth one careful attempt:

  • Soak in cool water with an oxygen-based cleaner (not chlorine bleach, which weakens fibres and yellows acrylic) for several hours to overnight.
  • Rinse fully and dry flat.
  • Repeat once. If two attempts do nothing, stop — further attempts damage the fabric more than the stain does.

Do not attempt this on untreated wool or anything you have not identified. See the fibre test in how to wash it.

What most storage guides get wrong

They recommend airtight plastic containers, which is exactly backwards. Airtight is good for documents and bad for textiles, because textiles need to exchange moisture with the air around them.

They skip the pre-wash on the reasoning that a clean-looking item is clean. Invisible residue is the main cause of the brown staining people discover twenty years later, and by then it is permanent.

They suggest the loft, which is the single worst microclimate in a house.

They treat vacuum bags as space-saving with no downside. For crochet the compression permanently flattens the stitch texture, which is the thing that made it worth keeping.

Frequently asked questions

Should I wash a baby blanket before storing it? Yes, always, even if it looks clean. Invisible oils oxidise into permanent brown stains.

Can I store a handmade blanket in a plastic box? Not sealed. If it is the only option, leave the lid loose, add no packing, and keep it indoors — but a cardboard box or cotton bag is genuinely better.

Is the loft a bad place to store keepsakes? Yes — the worst common choice. Temperature swings, humidity swings and pests.

How often should I check on it? Every year or two. Unfold, look for insect damage and discolouration, refold along different lines.

Do I need acid-free tissue? It helps over decades and is cheap. A clean cotton pillowcase is a reasonable substitute. Ordinary gift tissue is acidic — do not use it.

What about a shadow box or frame? Lovely for booties, and only with UV-filtering glass, an acid-free mount, and out of direct sun. Framing without UV protection fades an item within a few years.
