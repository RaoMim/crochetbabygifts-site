How to Wash a Crochet Baby Blanket Without Ruining It

If the yarn was acrylic or a cotton blend: machine wash cold or warm on a gentle cycle with a mild detergent, then tumble dry on low. That is most handmade baby blankets and it is genuinely that simple.

If the yarn was untreated wool or alpaca: do not put it in the machine. Hand wash cool, do not wring, and dry flat. One warm machine cycle will felt it permanently and there is no way back.

Everything else is detail — but two details cause almost all the ruined blankets, so they are worth two minutes.

First: find out what it is made of

If you made it, you know. If it was a gift, ask — and if you cannot ask, treat it as wool. The cautious method never damages acrylic; the acrylic method destroys wool.

A test if you truly cannot find out: wet a small area at a corner with warm water and rub it firmly between your fingers for thirty seconds. Untreated wool will start to felt slightly and feel denser and fuzzier. Acrylic, cotton and superwash will feel exactly the same as before.

The two mistakes that cause permanent damage

1. Lifting it while it is wet

This is the big one and it applies to every fibre, including acrylic.

A wet blanket is extremely heavy and crochet fabric has a lot of give. Picking a soaking blanket up by one corner stretches it out of shape under its own weight, and the distortion often does not come out. A 30-inch blanket can come out of the machine and end up 34 inches long and 26 wide.

Instead: support the whole thing. Gather it into a bundle and lift it with both hands underneath, or lift it inside a mesh laundry bag, or press the water out against the side of the sink and roll it in a towel before moving it anywhere.

2. Heat on wool, or hot water on anything

Untreated wool felts with heat and agitation together. There is no repair — the fibres have physically locked. This is the single most common way a handmade blanket is destroyed, and it is nearly always someone being helpful.

Acrylic does not felt, but sustained high heat can flatten and glaze the fibre, leaving the fabric permanently stiffer and shinier. Tumble dry low, not hot.

Machine washing, step by step

  1. Use a mesh laundry bag or a zipped duvet cover. This is the whole trick. It stops the blanket snagging on the drum, stops it wrapping around other items, and gives you something to lift it out by.
  2. Wash it alone, or with soft items only. No zips, no hooks, no denim — anything with hardware will snag and pull a stitch into a long loop.
  3. Mild detergent, and less than you think. Fragrance-free and dye-free for a baby item. Detergent residue is a more common cause of skin irritation than the yarn.
  4. No fabric softener. It coats fibres, reduces absorbency, and over time makes acrylic feel greasy rather than soft. If the blanket is stiff, see how to soften it — softener is not the fix.
  5. Gentle cycle, low spin. A high spin is agitation, which is the thing you are trying to limit.

Drying it flat, properly

Flat drying is what keeps the shape, and it is worth doing for any blanket you care about even when the label allows a dryer.

  1. Lay a large dry towel down. Put the blanket on it, roll the two together like a swiss roll, and press. This removes a surprising amount of water without wringing.
  2. Unroll, move to a dry towel or a drying rack.
  3. Square it up now — gently pat and nudge it to its intended dimensions while damp. Measure if the size matters. This is the moment the finished shape is set.
  4. Leave it flat, turning once. A thick blanket takes a day or two.

Do not hang it over a line or a rail while wet. Same problem as lifting it: the weight pulls it long.

Stains

Milk, formula and spit-up: cold water first, always. Warm water sets protein stains. Rinse from the back of the fabric, then wash normally.

Anything oily: a drop of washing-up liquid worked in gently with a finger, left ten minutes, then washed.

Do not use chlorine bleach on anything. It weakens fibres, yellows acrylic, and destroys wool outright. Oxygen bleach is safe on cotton and acrylic if you need it.

What most care guides get wrong

They organise by “handmade” rather than by fibre. Handmade is not a material. An acrylic blanket and an alpaca blanket need opposite treatment and the only thing they have in common is the maker.

They say “hand wash only” for everything, to be safe. This is bad advice, because it means the blanket goes in a cupboard instead of being used, which is worse than a small amount of wear.

They almost never mention the lifting problem, which distorts more blankets than hot water does.

Frequently asked questions

Can I put a crochet baby blanket in the washing machine? If it is acrylic, cotton, a blend, or superwash wool — yes, gentle cycle, in a mesh bag. Untreated wool and alpaca, no.

What temperature should I wash it at? Cold or warm, up to about 30 °C / 86 °F. Hot water offers no benefit here and carries all the risk.

Can I tumble dry it? Acrylic, cotton and blends: yes, on low. Wool of any kind, bamboo and viscose: no.

How do I stop it stretching? Never lift it by a corner while wet, never hang it wet, and square it up while damp.

How often should a baby blanket be washed? As often as it needs it. This is why washable yarn matters — the answer with a newborn is “constantly”.

My blanket went stiff after washing. Can I fix it? Usually. Wash again with no detergent to remove residue, then dry flat. Repeated washing also softens acrylic over time. Fabric softener is not the answer.
