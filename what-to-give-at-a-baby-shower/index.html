What to Give at a Baby Shower When You Can't Crochet

Give the thing they will run out of, in the size nobody else buys, and put a real note with it. That is the whole method, and it beats a handmade item chosen badly.

Most baby shower advice is a list of products. This is not that. It is the three questions that decide whether a gift gets used or ends up in the returns pile, and they are worth more than any list.

Question 1: will they run out of this?

New parents are given an enormous quantity of newborn-sized clothes and almost nothing for six months onward. They are given decorative items in abundance and consumables almost never.

The gifts that get used are the ones that get used up. Nappies, wipes, muslin squares, bibs, vests, nappy cream, laundry detergent for sensitive skin. These feel unglamorous to give, which is exactly why nobody else gives them, and it is why they land.

The single most reliably appreciated version of this: nappies in size 2 and 3, not newborn. Newborn size lasts a few weeks and everyone buys it. Size 3 arrives just as the supply crisis does.

Question 2: is it the size or season nobody thought about?

Look at when the baby is actually due, then add six months, and dress that child.

A baby born in November will be nine months old the following August and will need summer clothes in 9–12 month size — which nobody at a November shower is buying. That gift arrives when the cupboard is empty and gets remembered.

Sizes to give: 6–9 months and 9–12 months. Sizes to avoid: newborn and 0–3, which will be overflowing.

Question 3: does it require them to do anything?

A gift that needs assembling, registering, hand-washing, or a decision about whether they even want it is a small task disguised as a present. New parents have no capacity for tasks.

Things that are secretly tasks: anything needing batteries you did not include, anything requiring a size or colour choice later, anything hand-wash only, and — gently — anything that arrives as an IOU.

The handmade-feeling gift without the handmade

You want the gift to feel considered rather than ordered. Three things do that, and none of them require a hook.

Presentation. A stack of plain muslin squares tied with a wide ribbon and a handwritten tag reads as thoughtful. The same squares in the delivery box do not. Presentation is where “handmade feeling” actually comes from, and it costs a ribbon.

The note. Write something specific rather than “congratulations”. A sentence about the parent — what they will be good at, what you are looking forward to — is the part they keep. People keep notes and forget objects.

One consumable plus one keepsake. The consumable gets used and appreciated in week two. The keepsake gets appreciated in year five. Together they cover both, and the pairing is what makes a modest gift feel complete.

If you want a handmade item, buy one from someone who made it

There is a real difference between a mass-produced blanket and one a person crocheted, and you can give the second without making it. Independent makers sell finished baby items — blankets, hats, booties, small toys — and buying one supports someone doing the work properly.

Two things to check before you buy a handmade baby item:

  • Ask whether it is machine washable. Many handmade items are not, and an unwashable blanket for a newborn is a decorative object. See the yarn guide for why this matters more than anything else.
  • Check nothing is sewn on. Buttons, beads, ribbons and stitched-on eyes can be pulled free. Items where the detail is crocheted in are the safe ones.

What to give, by budget

Budget The reliable choice Why it works
Under $15 Muslin squares, tied, with a written note Endlessly useful, impossible to have too many
$15–30 Nappies in size 2–3, plus one nice thing Solves a real shortage nobody else addresses
$30–60 Clothes in 6–12 month size, or a handmade hat from an independent maker Fills the gap everyone leaves
$60–120 A handmade blanket from a maker, machine washable The keepsake, done properly
Any A wearable blanket or sleep sack The one warm item that is safe for a sleeping baby

That last row is worth explaining. Current safe-sleep guidance keeps loose blankets out of the crib for the first year, so a wearable blanket is the item that covers the sleeping baby safely. Almost nobody gives one. It is the most practically useful warm gift on the list. The safe-sleep page has the detail.

What gets quietly returned

Said plainly, because avoiding these is half the job:

  • Newborn clothes. They already have more than the baby can wear before outgrowing them.
  • Anything hand-wash only, for a newborn.
  • Loose crib bedding sets — cot bumpers, quilts, decorative pillows. They cannot be used in the sleep space.
  • Toys for a much older child. Well-meant, and it will be stored for two years and lost.
  • Strongly themed decorative items. They are furnishing a room to their own taste.
  • Anything perfumed. Newborn skin and scented products do not mix.

What most gift guides get wrong

They optimise for how the gift looks when opened, at a party, in front of people. The gifts that matter are the ones that are useful in week three, when nobody is watching.

They assume newborn size. The baby is newborn for about six weeks and a baby for two years.

They ignore safe sleep entirely and cheerfully recommend crib bedding sets that current guidance says not to use.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I spend on a baby shower gift? For a friend or colleague, $25–50 is normal. For close family, more. A $20 gift chosen well beats a $60 gift chosen badly, and the note is free.

Is it rude to give nappies as a gift? No — it is often the most appreciated thing in the room, and the parents will tell you so a month later. Present them nicely and add one small keepsake alongside.

What if there is a registry? Buy from it. A registry is the parents telling you what they need; going off-list is choosing your preference over theirs. If you want to add something personal, add the note and the presentation.

Can I give a handmade gift if I’m not crafty? Yes. Buy the item from a maker and handle the presentation and the note yourself. That is where the personal part lives anyway.

What is the safest warm gift? A wearable blanket or sleep sack, in size 6–12 months rather than newborn.
