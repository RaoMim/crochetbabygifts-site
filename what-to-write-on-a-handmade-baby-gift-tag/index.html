What to Write on a Handmade Baby Gift Tag

Three lines: what it is made of, how to wash it, and what it is for. Then, if you want, one personal sentence. That order is deliberate — the practical information is what gets the gift used, and the sentiment is what gets it kept.

Most tag advice is entirely sentiment. That is the wrong way round.

The three practical lines

Line 1 — what it is made of

“100% cotton.” “Acrylic.” “Superwash merino.”

Boring, and the most durable thing on the tag. In fifteen years someone will be deciding how to store or wash this, and the fibre will be the question. Nobody remembers.

Line 2 — how to wash it

“Machine wash warm, tumble dry low.” “Machine wash cold, dry flat.” “Hand wash cool, dry flat.”

Be specific. “Wash with care” tells the recipient nothing and defaults them to never washing it, which means never using it. If your item genuinely is machine-washable and dryer-safe, say so plainly — it is the single most reassuring thing you can write.

Line 3 — what it is for

For a blanket:

“For tummy time, the pram and the sofa — and for the big bed once they’re over one.”

This exists because current infant safe-sleep guidance keeps loose blankets out of the crib for the first year, and a new parent handed a beautiful crib-sized blanket has to work out what to do with that. Answering it yourself is the most useful line on the tag. Detail in are crochet blankets safe for babies.

For other items it is simpler — “Fits around 3–6 months” on a hat does the same job, because it tells them when to look for it rather than trying it once and putting it away.

Then the personal line

One sentence. Specific beats effusive, and something about the parent lands better than something generic about the baby.

Made-with-love, done without cliché - Made for you, one row at a time, through a very long February. - Every stitch of this was made thinking about you three. - This took me six weeks and I would do it again.

About the parents rather than the baby - You are going to be so much better at this than you think. - She is very lucky, and she does not know it yet. - You have wanted this for a long time. Here it is.

Plain and warm - Something soft for someone new. - For the first cold morning. - Welcome, small person.

For a keepsake framing - Made in 2026 for your first winter. - Made by your great-aunt Nadia, who cannot sew but can crochet. - Keep this one. It is the first thing anyone made for you.

When you do not know the parents well - Congratulations — I hope this gets used until it falls apart. - Handmade, machine washable, no ceremony required.

That last one is worth noticing: explicitly giving permission to use it roughly is a kindness. The most common reason a handmade gift goes unused is that the recipient thinks it is too nice.

Where to put it

Tie it to the ribbon on the wrapping, never to the item. Anything attached to something a baby will use is an entanglement risk, which is why donation programmes ban ties and ribbons on the item itself.

If you want the care information to survive the wrapping being thrown away — and you do — write those three lines on a second small card and tuck it inside, or include the yarn’s own label, which carries the fibre and the care symbols already.

What most tag guides get wrong

They are 100% sentiment. Forty variations on “made with love” and not one mention of the fibre or the wash. The care line is the one that determines whether the gift is used at all.

They suggest attaching the tag to the item with ribbon, which is the one place it should not go.

They recommend long messages. A tag is not a card. Three practical lines and one personal sentence is the whole format; if you have more to say, write a card and put it in the parcel.

They never mention giving permission to use it. For handmade gifts that single move probably does more good than any wording choice.

Frequently asked questions

What do you write on a handmade gift tag? Fibre, wash instructions, what it is for, then one personal sentence.

Should I include care instructions on a gift? Yes — it is the most useful thing on the tag, and it is what keeps the item in use.

How long should a gift tag message be? One sentence of sentiment. The practical lines are three short fragments.

Do I put my name on it? Worth doing, especially for a keepsake — “Made by … , 2026.” In twenty years it is the difference between a nice blanket and a family object.

What if I do not know the parents well? Keep it warm and brief, and lean on the practical lines. “Congratulations — I hope this gets used until it falls apart” works for anyone.

Can I write on the yarn label instead? You can include it — it carries the fibre and care symbols — but write your own tag too. The label has no personal line and no “what it is for”.
